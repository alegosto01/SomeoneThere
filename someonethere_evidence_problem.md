## Demand exists because Madrid keeps attracting newcomers who cannot inspect in person

Madrid is a large inbound city for exactly the kinds of people SomeoneThere targets. The **City of Madrid** had **3,527,924 residents** on 1 January 2025. In 2024 alone, it recorded **135,718 registrations arriving from abroad** and **96,869 arrivals from other Spanish municipalities**. Its **external migration balance was +123,267**, while internal migration was negative, meaning international inflows were the main driver of population growth. The same city source says **20% of residents are foreign nationals** and **more than 30% were born abroad**. citeturn24view0

At the **regional** level, the pattern is similar. INE reported that **Comunidad de Madrid had net external migration of 113,964 people in 2024**, one of the highest figures in Spain. That matters because even if only a fraction of those movers are looking for rentals remotely, the annual funnel is already large. citeturn6search0turn6search1

The student segment is also large enough to justify a specialized service. Official university statistics show **36,463 foreign students** enrolled in the university system in **Comunidad de Madrid** in **2022–2023**. Savills, using Ministry of Universities data, reports that **Madrid province had 331,908 on-site university enrolments** across bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral studies in **2023–2024**, the highest student concentration in Spain. The same report notes that **31.4% of undergraduates in on-site Spanish universities study outside their home province**, which is important because domestic mobility also creates remote-housing decisions, not only international mobility. citeturn3search2turn13view0

Madrid is also one of Spain’s main Erasmus destinations. Savills reports that Spain hosted **about 55,700 Erasmus students** in **2021–2022**, and that **Madrid attracted 17.7%** of them. That implies roughly **9,859 Erasmus students in Madrid** for that academic cycle. Even if your initial customer base is only a small share of this inflow, it is already material for an MVP. citeturn13view0turn25calculator0

The most useful target-market statistics for your deck are therefore not abstract population totals, but **newcomer flows**, **foreign-student counts**, **Erasmus inflows**, and **students studying away from home**. Together, they show a consistent stream of people who are likely to be choosing housing from a distance. citeturn24view0turn6search0turn3search2turn13view0

## The amount of money at risk before move-in is already large enough to justify a verifier

SomeoneThere becomes much easier to justify when you quantify the euro exposure **before** a tenant has keys. On the student side, UCM’s institutional factsheet says that renting a **room in a shared apartment in Madrid** typically ranges from **€300 to €700 per month**, with an **average around €475 per month**. UC3M’s international factsheet separately estimates total **living expenses in Madrid**, including a shared apartment, food, and transportation, at roughly **€700–€800 per month**. citeturn29view1turn29view0

Portal data suggests the room market can be even tighter than university guidance implies. Idealista reported that the average advertised room rent in **Madrid city** was **€565 per month** in 2024, while Fotocasa put **Madrid capital** at **€605 per month** in early 2025. Those figures are not identical because methodologies and dates differ, but they point in the same direction: even a single room often involves a substantial commitment for students and young workers. citeturn15search11turn15search12

For full flats, Idealista reported that **Comunidad de Madrid** reached **€21.7 per square metre per month** in **June 2026**, the highest regional asking rent in Spain. At that asking-rent level, a **45 m²** flat implies roughly **€976.50 per month**, and a **60 m²** flat implies roughly **€1,302 per month**. citeturn19search9turn25calculator1turn25calculator2

Spanish law makes the upfront cash exposure even clearer. The **Urban Leases Act** requires a **one-month deposit** for housing leases and permits an **additional guarantee of up to two more months** in many residential contracts. Separately, the **2023 Housing Law** made real-estate management and contract-formalization costs the landlord’s responsibility in the cases covered by the law, which means you should be careful about assuming tenant-paid agency fees in standard long-term housing. But even **without** agency fees, a renter may still be asked for **first month + one month deposit + up to two months additional guarantee**. In plain terms, lawful upfront exposure can reach **four months of rent before move-in**. citeturn14search1turn14search2

At Madrid’s June 2026 regional asking-rent level, four months on a **45 m²** flat is about **€3,906**, and four months on a **60 m²** flat is about **€5,208**. Even at UCM’s **€475 average room** estimate, two months is **€950** and four months is **€1,900**. If SomeoneThere costs a small fraction of the money being committed, the economic logic becomes intuitive. citeturn33calculator0turn33calculator1turn33calculator2turn33calculator3turn29view1turn19search9

## Scam evidence is real, but the strongest proof is the repeated warning pattern

A clean official statistic for “number of Madrid rental scams per year” was not easy to locate in the sources reviewed. What **is** very clear is the warning pattern across public cybersecurity, law-enforcement, and university channels. INCIBE states that **fraudulent rental ads on the internet have increased in recent years**, often involving non-existent homes or listings designed to extract money from users. citeturn8search2

The **Guardia Civil** gives highly specific prevention advice for rental bookings: check other users’ feedback, be suspicious of very low prices, **ask to see the property by video call**, avoid sending document photos, and **avoid prepayments by wire transfer**. Those are not abstract warnings; they map directly onto SomeoneThere’s value proposition of a live, permission-based, local verification step before money is sent. citeturn26search1

Madrid universities also treat the issue as real enough to warn incoming students explicitly. UC3M’s “How to avoid and recognize a rental scam” guide says the university **usually sees several students per year** who encounter scams. It advises students **not to pay any amount before arriving to Madrid**, warns against paying or signing before viewing, flags mismatched photos and addresses, cautions against transfers through services such as Western Union or MoneyGram, and notes that because of **housing scarcity in Madrid**, students may receive emails offering **non-existent accommodation**. citeturn31view0

UCM’s institutional factsheet gives the same general signal from another angle: incoming students are told to use **reliable platforms**, do careful research before finalizing agreements, and learn how student accommodation works in Madrid to avoid **potential issues like scams**. That is important because it shows that the trust problem is being recognized by host institutions, not only by police or cybersecurity agencies. citeturn29view1

For your presentation, this means you should probably **not** anchor on a single “X scams per year in Madrid” claim unless you later find a strong primary source. A better, more defensible narrative is that **official and university sources repeatedly warn remote renters about exactly the behaviors SomeoneThere is built to reduce**: pressure to pay before viewing, fake urgency, bad identity signals, misleading photos, and non-existent or misrepresented properties. citeturn8search2turn26search1turn31view0turn29view1

## The deeper pain point is remote renting under weak trust

The trust problem is broader than fraud. UCM tells incoming students that the most common routes are **shared rooms and student residences**, and specifically recommends caution and research before finalizing agreements. UC3M goes even further and says students should **secure housing before arriving in Madrid**, while also linking them to anti-scam guidance. Together, these sources show the core SomeoneThere problem clearly: many people need to make housing decisions from abroad or from another city, but they do not have anyone physically present to verify the reality on the ground. citeturn29view0turn29view1

UC3M’s scam guide also makes an important distinction that helps your positioning: it advises against paying before viewing not only because of outright fraud, but also because otherwise a renter may arrive to find **unexpected surprises** related to the apartment’s **condition, contract, or location**. That is exactly where SomeoneThere can differentiate itself. A flat can be real and still be a bad remote decision because the entryway is poor, the street is noisy, the room is smaller than implied, the furniture is different, or the building condition undermines confidence. citeturn31view0

This is why the service should be presented as a **verification layer**, not a “scam detector.” Existing safety advice in the sources reviewed centers on using reliable platforms, doing video calls, and being careful before payment. I did not find evidence in the official Madrid-university material reviewed of a standard, widely promoted option where an independent local person attends the viewing on the renter’s behalf and produces a structured verification summary. That suggests a real service gap, though it is better framed as an inference from reviewed sources than as a measured market statistic. citeturn29view0turn29view1turn31view0turn26search1

## Madrid’s market pressure makes rushed decisions more likely

Madrid is not just expensive; it is fast. Idealista reported that in **Q4 2025**, rental listings in **Madrid** received an average of **44 contacts per ad** before they were taken down. In **Q3 2025**, the figure was **46 contacts per ad**. That is a useful metric because it captures market competition directly: newcomers are not evaluating one quiet option at a time; they are competing with dozens of other households for the same listing. citeturn19search1turn19search10

Listings also disappear quickly. Idealista’s rental “express” analysis indicates that in the large-city segment, **13% of rentals in Madrid** at the end of 2024 were leaving the market in **under 24 hours**. When a city combines high competition with rapid listing turnover, renters are more likely to feel pressure to decide—and to decide remotely. citeturn19search11turn19search7

The longer-term supply-demand trend is also moving the wrong way for renters. Idealista/data reported that between **March 2020 and January 2025**, Madrid’s rental listing stock fell **46.7%**, while relative rental demand rose **290%**. In the same comparison, asking rents in Madrid reached **€21.2/m²** by February 2025. That is a strong “market urgency” statistic because it links tighter supply to rising prices and rising competition in the same city. citeturn1search3

For the student segment, the structural shortage is visible in purpose-built accommodation too. Savills reports that Madrid has **24.2% of Spain’s student-housing beds**, but even in this major university hub, the provision rate is only **7.8%** relative to students. The same report describes a national bed-to-student ratio of about **1:13** and a large mismatch between demand and supply. In other words, even specialized student accommodation does not fully absorb demand; many students still spill into the private rental and room market where SomeoneThere is relevant. citeturn13view0

This is one of the best sections for the SomeoneThere story because it explains why people may feel forced to send money quickly. A pressured market does not only create higher prices; it creates **bad decision conditions**. That makes a fast, local, independent viewing service much easier to justify. citeturn19search10turn19search11turn1search3turn13view0

## The downside is not only losing a deposit

If a remote booking goes wrong, the cost can escalate quickly beyond the original transfer. UCM explicitly states that exchange students are responsible for **finding and paying for their own accommodation and living expenses**, which means the financial and logistical burden of a failed housing choice sits directly with the student and family. citeturn29view1

A practical proxy for emergency replacement costs is hotel pricing. INE reports that hotels in Spain invoiced an average **ADR of €127.7 in 2025**, and **€123.7 per occupied room in May 2026**. Using the May 2026 figure as a simple proxy, **three nights** of emergency hotel stay would cost about **€371**, and **five nights** about **€619**. Compared with UC3M’s benchmark of **€700–€800 monthly living expenses** for an exchange student in Madrid, three hotel nights would consume roughly **53% of a €700 budget**, and five nights would absorb roughly **77% of an €800 budget**. citeturn20search4turn20search5turn34calculator0turn34calculator1turn34calculator2turn34calculator3turn29view0

That means a failed remote rental can create a chain of costs: **lost or frozen upfront payment, temporary accommodation, local transport, extra search time, and stress during arrival week**. Even if you do not quantify every component perfectly, the economic logic is persuasive. SomeoneThere helps renters avoid the much more expensive scenario in which they learn the truth only after arrival or after sending money. citeturn29view1turn20search4turn29view0
